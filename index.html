What is a zone of inhibition?

A zone of inhibition is the clear area around an antiseptic or antibiotic disc on an agar plate, where bacteria cannot grow.

Dotted area: bacteria growing. Clear circles: zones of inhibition.

Why is the area clear? The antiseptic soaks out of the paper disc into the agar. Close to the disc it is strong enough to kill the bacteria or stop them reproducing, so nothing grows there.

What does the size tell you? A bigger zone means the antiseptic is more effective against that bacterium. In the diagram, A is the most effective and B is the least.

No clear zone means the substance has no effect on that bacterium. The water disc is the control, so it should never have a zone.

Pay close attention: part 2 of 2Dr Rickard asked you to learn how to calculate the area. Follow these four steps every time, in this order.

How to calculate the area

  1. Measure the diameter twice, at right angles to each other, in millimetres (mm). Zones are not always perfect circles.
  2. Find the mean diameter: add the two diameters and divide by 2.
  3. Find the radius: halve the mean diameter.
  4. Calculate the area: area = πr2. Use the π button on your calculator. The unit is mm2.

Mistakes that lose marks
  • Using the diameter in πr2 instead of the radius.
  • Doing π × r × 2 instead of π × r × r.
  • Writing mm instead of mm2 for an area.

The required practical

Investigating the effect of antiseptics on the growth of bacteria. Exam questions often ask for a step and the reason for it, so learn both columns.

Part 1: prepare a plate of bacteria
1. Sterilise the Petri dish and agar in an autoclave, or use dishes bought already sterile.
Why: kills any microorganisms already there.
2. Pour the sterile agar into the dish and let it set.
Why: agar gives the bacteria the nutrients they need to grow.
3. Pass the inoculating loop through a Bunsen flame until it glows red, then let it cool.
Why: kills microorganisms on the loop. Cooling stops it killing the bacteria you want to grow.
4. Spread the bacteria evenly over the agar. Lift the lid only slightly while you do this.
Why: gives an even layer of bacteria (a “lawn”). Lifting the lid slightly stops microorganisms from the air getting in.
Part 2: test the antiseptics
5. Soak paper discs in different antiseptics, or in different concentrations of one antiseptic. Soak one disc in sterile water.
Why: the water disc is the control. It shows that any clear zone is caused by the antiseptic, not the paper disc.
6. Use sterile forceps to place the discs on the agar, spaced well apart.
Why: sterile forceps stop contamination. Spacing stops the zones overlapping.
7. Secure the lid with two strips of tape. Do not seal it all the way round.
Why: stops microorganisms getting in, but lets oxygen in so harmful anaerobic bacteria do not grow.
8. Label the dish and store it upside down.
Why: the label tells you what is growing. Upside down stops condensation dripping onto the agar.
9. Incubate at 25 °C for about 48 hours.
Why: 25 °C reduces the chance of growing pathogens. Hospital laboratories use 37 °C (body temperature) so bacteria grow faster.
10. Measure the diameter of each clear zone twice and calculate its area.
Why: the bigger the zone, the more effective the antiseptic.
The variables
Independent
The type (or concentration) of antiseptic. This is what you change.
Dependent
The area of the zone of inhibition. This is what you measure.
Control
Kept the same: type of bacterium, size of disc, volume of antiseptic, incubation temperature and time.
Words that are easy to mix up
Antiseptic
A chemical used on living tissue, such as skin, to kill microorganisms or stop them growing.
Disinfectant
A chemical used on non-living surfaces, such as kitchen worktops.
Antibiotic
A medicine that kills bacteria inside the body. Antibiotics do not work against viruses.
Aseptic technique
Ways of working that stop unwanted microorganisms getting into a culture.
